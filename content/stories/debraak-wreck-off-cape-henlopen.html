On the afternoon of May 25, 1798, the British warship DeBraak capsized and sank off Cape Henlopen. She had been escorting and protecting a convoy of British and American merchant ships bound for the United States when a sudden squall caught her.

The loss

DeBraak was commanded by James Drew of the Royal Navy, and he was among those lost. How many died with him is one of the details on which accounts disagree. A state historical marker gives thirty-three of an eighty-five-man crew. Other accounts put it at thirty-five crew plus a dozen Spanish prisoners on board, or at forty-seven lost in all. The local pilot, Andrew Allen, survived, according to the historical marker.

The disagreement is worth saying out loud rather than smoothing over. A wreck two and a quarter centuries old reaches us through records that do not always agree, and this one is no exception.

Raised in 1986

For nearly two hundred years the wreck stayed on the bottom off Lewes. In August 1986 a private salvage company raised the hull from the ocean floor. The raising was a commercial operation, and it badly damaged what was left of the ship.

Altogether more than 20,000 artifacts were recovered from the wreck site, along with roughly a third of the ship's lower hull.

Delaware's collection

The state held passive custody of the collection from 1986 and in 1992 bought it outright from the salvage company. Since then the remaining section of the hull and the artifact collection have been curated by the Delaware Division of Historical and Cultural Affairs. The hull itself is kept at a conservation facility near Cape Henlopen State Park.

The Division's Zwaanendael Museum, at 102 Kings Highway in Lewes, has made the DeBraak a recurring subject of its exhibitions and programs over the years.

Seeing it

The Division runs free "Walk to the DeBraak" tours of the hull, for groups of up to 18 visitors ages 10 and up. Dates are set by the Division; the Zwaanendael Museum is the place to ask about the next one.
