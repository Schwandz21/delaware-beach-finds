Caesar Rodney, Myth and Record

In His Boots and Spurs

The most famous sentence about Caesar Rodney's ride was written thirty-seven years later, by a man correcting somebody else's version of events.

By First State History DeskAugust 19, 20269 min readDelaware History

The most famous sentence about the most famous ride in Delaware history was written thirty-seven years after it happened, by a man correcting somebody else's version of events.

In September 1813, Thomas McKean sat down to write to Caesar Augustus Rodney — nephew of the signer, and by then a former Attorney General of the United States. The nephew had asked about July 1776, and had got some of it wrong. McKean's reply opens by saying so: it was not, he wrote, as you have conceived.

Then he supplied the detail that has outlived everything else in the letter. He had met Rodney at the State House door, McKean recalled, in his boots and spurs, as the members were assembling.

Boots and spurs. Two words that put a man on a horse, at speed, arriving in the nick of time. Almost every retelling of Caesar Rodney's ride since has been built on them.

They are also a memory, written down after nearly four decades, by a participant with a stake in how the story was told, in a letter whose stated purpose was to correct a legend that had already started forming.

What is documented

Start with what is not in question.

In the first days of July 1776, Delaware's three-man delegation to the Continental Congress was split. Thomas McKean was for independence. George Read was against. Caesar Rodney was in Kent County, roughly seventy or eighty miles south, on business connected to loyalist unrest in Sussex. A tied delegation could not cast a colony's vote.

McKean sent word. Rodney travelled to Philadelphia. He arrived on July 2 and Delaware's vote went for independence. All of that is documented, and none of it is seriously disputed. Rodney signed the Declaration. His arrival mattered.

Now start listing what we would like to know and cannot establish with the same confidence.

How far, and by what

The distance is not settled. The National Park Service describes the journey as “nearly 80 miles.” Other standard accounts give seventy. That is a ten-mile spread on the single most-repeated number in the story, between sources that are both reputable, and neither figure comes from Rodney.

The mode is looser still. The image is a man on horseback for the whole distance. Some accounts allow that parts of the journey may have been made by carriage. McKean's letter puts Rodney at the door in boots and spurs; it does not describe eighty miles in the saddle, because it is a sentence about an arrival, not an itinerary.

The weather has the same shape. A thunderstorm appears in nearly every version, and a July storm in the mid-Atlantic is entirely plausible. But the vivid business — the all-night ride timed to the minute, the doorway entrance as the roll is called — is the accumulated product of two centuries of retelling, not a description anyone wrote down in 1776.

None of this makes the ride false. It makes the ride documented in outline and decorated in detail, which is a different and more ordinary thing than the version on the postcard.

Where the picture came from

If the record is thin, the imagery is not, and it is possible to date most of it.

In 1923, on the Fourth of July, Wilmington dedicated James Edward Kelly's bronze equestrian statue in what is now Rodney Square. That statue is where the modern mental image largely comes from: horse mid-stride, rider leaning forward, urgency cast in metal. It is a work of art made a hundred and forty-seven years after the event it depicts.

In 1969 the musical 1776 reached Broadway, and the 1972 film carried it further. In 1999 the United States Mint put Rodney on horseback on the Delaware state quarter, and several hundred million of them went into circulation. For most Americans who can picture Caesar Rodney at all, the picture is Kelly's statue, by way of a coin.

This is how civic memory usually works. It is not a scandal. It is worth naming, because a state that describes itself by its history is entitled to know which parts are record and which parts are portraiture.

Why Delaware needed the ride

Here is the question the whole series has been circling.

Delaware spent most of the colonial period without a clear identity of its own. It was the Three Lower Counties, settled by Swedes and Dutch before the English, handed to William Penn because Pennsylvania needed the river mouth, legislating separately from 1704 while sharing Pennsylvania's governor for seventy-two years. Its northern boundary is a circular arc drawn from a courthouse cupola because a proprietor's grant said twelve miles and nobody thought hard about what that meant on the ground. It became a state in 1776 by breaking from two authorities at once, and first among states in 1787 because equal representation in the Senate solved its central problem and it saw no reason to deliberate.

That is a history of accumulation, accident, borrowed institutions and small-state arithmetic. It is genuinely interesting. It is not, in the ordinary sense, heroic.

A man riding through a storm to break a deadlock is heroic. It is a founding scene with a protagonist, a clock and weather — the kind of thing other states got from battles Delaware did not host. Of course it became the story. It is the only moment in the state's founding that looks like the founding stories everyone else was telling.

What the series has been about

Across nine installments a pattern keeps repeating. Delaware's borders came from a dispute nobody finished cleanly. Its separate legislature came from an inability to sit with Pennsylvania. Its statehood came in a summer of double separation. Its primacy came from moving fast on a compromise that protected it. And in 1865, asked to abolish slavery for fewer than eighteen hundred people, its legislature refused, and freedom arrived from outside.

None of that is a story about destiny. It is a story about a small jurisdiction repeatedly deciding, under pressure, what it was going to be — sometimes admirably, sometimes not.

The ride belongs in that account, and it survives the scrutiny. A delegate crossed the state under difficult conditions because a vote could not be cast without him, and it was cast. That is true, it is consequential, and it needs no thunderclap.

What the ride does not need to carry is the whole weight of Delaware's founding. The state was assembled over a century and a half by surveyors, assemblies, tavern conventions, arguments about river access and one very consequential clause about the Senate. Rodney's night on the road is the last mile of a much longer journey, and the least representative one.

The letter, again

Go back to McKean, in 1813, writing to a nephew who had the story slightly wrong.

He was in his seventies. He had been President of Congress, Chief Justice, Governor of Pennsylvania. He had watched the Revolution become a subject that people were starting to get wrong in public, and he was trying, in a private letter, to fix one family's version of one week.

Two centuries later the sentence he wrote to correct a legend is the primary evidence for it.

Delaware could do worse for a founding document than that: not a proclamation, not a monument, but an old man writing down what he remembered, insisting it was not quite as you have conceived.

Sources: Thomas McKean to Caesar A. Rodney, 22 September 1813, in the Papers of Thomas Jefferson (Founders Online, U.S. National Archives), the source of the “boots and spurs” description; National Park Service materials on the Caesar Rodney statue and on Rodney's ride, which give the distance as “nearly 80 miles”; standard reference accounts giving the distance as 70 miles; U.S. Mint records for the 1999 Delaware state quarter. The distance discrepancy is left unresolved here because the sources genuinely disagree and no contemporaneous figure is known. A quotation about being “detained by thunder and rain,” sometimes attributed to Rodney himself, circulates widely online; it could not be traced to a verified primary document for this article and is therefore not quoted as his words. Mode of travel, timing and weather are described as tradition where the record does not support specificity.
